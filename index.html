Research

Guides

What photobiomodulation is

Photobiomodulation (PBM) is the use of light to produce a biological benefit. The widely used definition covers non-ionizing light from lasers, LEDs and broadband sources across the visible and infrared spectrum, so it is a broad field and not only red light. Red light therapy and near-infrared therapy are the best-studied forms, and they are the ones this app calculates for. Other wavelengths are absorbed by different molecules and are outside this calculator.

For red and near-infrared light, the usual explanation is absorption by chromophores inside mitochondria, including cytochrome c oxidase. The evidence for each use differs a lot, which is why the Research tab and the Evidence card label how strong it is.

Irradiance, time and dose

Irradiance is how strong the light is at the skin, in mW/cm². Dose (also called fluence) is the total energy delivered, in J/cm². The link is simple: dose = irradiance × time, with time in seconds and a factor of 1000 to convert milliwatts to watts.

Half the irradiance takes twice as long to reach the same dose. That is why the app offers a full power and a half power option for each protocol.

Distance and panel output

A panel is brightest close to the surface and fades with distance. It is not a point source, so the fall-off is gentler than the simple inverse-square rule and differs from panel to panel. That is why the app interpolates between your own readings at several distances instead of assuming a formula. Distances you did not measure are estimates, and the time range on each option widens to show that.

Red versus near-infrared, and how deep light goes

Red light (about 630 to 670 nm) is absorbed close to the surface, so it suits skin. Near-infrared (about 810 to 850 nm) travels farther, so it is used for deeper targets. Either way, most of the light is lost within the first centimeter or so. Drag the slider to see the app's rough estimate.

These percentages come from a simple model fitted to published ranges. Real tissue varies with fat, skin tone and blood flow.

The skull is a special case

Cadaver measurements find that only a small share of near-infrared light, on the order of a few percent or less, gets through scalp and skull. Several critical reviews argue that current devices may leave the cortex with very little light. Small trials report benefits anyway, so the picture is conflicting, and the app labels it that way.

More is not always better

Light therapy often shows a biphasic dose response: moderate doses can work better than very low or very high ones. That is why the dose slider stops at the top of each published window instead of letting you keep going.

Methylene blue and red light

Methylene blue (MB) is a dye and an old drug. In low doses it is thought to act as an electron cycler in mitochondria, the same machinery that red and near-infrared light is proposed to act on. Small human studies and a lot of animal work suggest effects on memory and brain activity, but the evidence is limited.

MB also absorbs red light, with a peak near 660 nm. That makes it a photosensitizer: with light and oxygen it makes reactive oxygen species, which is how photodynamic therapy kills bacteria in dental research. So pairing MB with a 660 nm panel is a different thing from using either one alone, and the research on it is mostly lab work plus one small pilot.

Safety: the FDA label for injectable methylene blue has a boxed warning for serotonin syndrome with drugs such as SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs and opioids, and it says not to use it in G6PD deficiency because of the risk of hemolytic anemia. This app does not recommend or dose methylene blue. Talk to a clinician before considering it.

Why panel numbers do not match

Solar power meters are built for sunlight and respond differently to narrow-band LEDs, so they tend to read high. Sources disagree on how much: an independent tester reports roughly 40 to 70% high, while a panel vendor reports 2 to 3 times. Some brands publish both a solar-meter number and a lab number, and the gap is about 2 times. The app applies a correction to solar-meter readings and shows the listed number next to it.

Peak and average also differ. A reading taken at the center is usually higher than the average across the panel, which is what the evenness test in the guided measurement shows.

How to read the evidence
  • Systematic review or meta-analysis: pools many studies, so it is the strongest kind of summary, but it is only as good as the trials inside it.
  • Human trial: a study in people. Small trials can be misleading.
  • Cadaver or lab measurement: shows how light behaves, not whether it helps anyone.
  • Modeling or simulation: a computer estimate of light spread.
  • Trial registry: a planned or ongoing study with no results yet.
  • Preprint: not peer reviewed yet.
  • Drug label: the official prescribing information, the authoritative place for warnings and approved uses.
  • Manufacturer-published: studies run, funded or hosted by the device maker. Many are done with universities, but the maker supplies the device and chooses what to publicize, so look for sham controls and independent authors.
  • Independent testing: measurements by third-party testers, usually of device output rather than health effects. Useful for checking claims, but methods vary and some sites earn commissions on sales.
  • News, vendor or guide: useful context, but not research, and sellers have a stake.
Safety basics
  • Eyes: 850 nm is invisible, and closing your eyes does not fully block it. Use goggles made for the wavelengths.
  • Ask a clinician first if you are pregnant (avoid directly over the abdomen), have active cancer or a suspicious lesion in the area, take photosensitizing medicines, have a photosensitive condition or epilepsy, have thyroid disease and want to treat the neck, or have implanted devices.
  • Skin: stop if you feel burning or irritation.
Myths and what the evidence says
Myth: More time under the light is always better.
The research describes a biphasic response, where very high doses can work worse than moderate ones.
Myth: A bigger number on the box means a better panel.
Many box numbers come from a solar meter, which reads high, and from the center of the panel at one distance.
Myth: Red light passes easily through the skull.
Cadaver studies find most of it is blocked, and critical reviews doubt that devices deliver much to the cortex.
Myth: Light will close a diastasis recti gap.
No studies of light for diastasis recti turned up. The evidence-based treatment is exercise, and even that evidence is low certainty.
Myth: You can trust any single study.
Look for systematic reviews, check the study type badge, and watch for preprints and vendor pages.
Glossary
  • PBM: photobiomodulation, the therapeutic use of non-ionizing light (lasers, LEDs or broadband) in the visible and infrared range.
  • RLT: red light therapy, the red and near-infrared form of PBM, which is what this app covers.
  • Irradiance: light power per area at the surface, in mW/cm².
  • Dose or fluence: energy per area, in J/cm².
  • NIR: near-infrared light, about 700 to 1100 nm.
  • tPBM: transcranial photobiomodulation, light applied through the scalp to the brain.
  • WALT: the World Association for Laser Therapy, which publishes dose recommendations in joules per treatment spot.
  • Peak versus average: the brightest spot on a panel versus the mean across its face.
  • Evenness: how uniform the light is across the panel face.

How to Use

Quick start

A one-minute tour of the app. It opens every time you start the app, right after the safety checklist the first time, until you tick Don't show this at startup again on it. You can turn it back on any time in Settings, General, under Quick Start, and Replay the Quick Start opens it whenever you like.

Install it on your phone
  • iPhone: open the app's web link in Safari, tap Share, then Add to Home Screen, then Add.
  • Android: open the link in Chrome, tap the menu (three dots), then Install app.
  • Open it once while online. After that it works without a connection.
  • Updates: a new version loads the next time you open the app while online. It can take two opens to show. The version number, App build, is at the very bottom of the page.
  • A file opened from a message or a file preview will not work on a phone. Use the web link.
Set up your panel
  • Panel Profile (top of the Calculator tab) is the panel every number is based on. Presets are built-in popular panels. My Presets are your own.
  • The line under the picker shows the panel's irradiance at 6 inches after the solar meter correction. The word estimated means 6 inches is outside your readings.
  • Edit opens the profile window. Add each wavelength in nm with the share of the panel's output it makes, then total irradiance readings (mW/cm²) at different distances with every wavelength on at 100% power. Tick Wavelengths fire together if red and near-infrared cannot be switched separately.
  • Solar meter correction: solar meters read high on LEDs. Use 0.5 for solar meter readings and 1.0 for spectrometer or lab numbers.
  • Measure My Panel (guided) is a button in the Edit window. It walks you through the readings, an optional red-only and near-infrared-only pair to find the wavelength split, and an optional 3 by 3 evenness test.
  • Built-in presets cannot be changed. Save as New Preset makes an editable copy.
  • Reading unit: choose mW/cm², W/m² or Btu/(ft²·h) to match your meter, in the Edit window or the guided measurement. The app converts to mW/cm² automatically (1 W/m² = 0.1 mW/cm², and 1 Btu/(ft²·h) = 0.3155 mW/cm²). Use a unit your meter shows without OL. Switching the unit converts the numbers you already typed.
  • More readings at more distances means better numbers. One reading means every other distance is a guess.
Choose a treatment and area
  • Pick the treatment first. The Target Area list then shows only the areas that make sense for it.
  • The app picks the wavelength from how deep the target is: red for shallow targets, both for in between, near-infrared for deep ones.
  • Each treatment has a published dose window in J/cm². The Evidence section lower on the page shows the status of the research, the sources, and any caution.
Read the recommendation
  • Recommended distance is how far to hold the panel from the skin.
  • Time goal and Distance: pick a time and the app finds the distance, or type a distance and the times update. Deep targets need a minimum irradiance at the surface, so the app can recommend a session shorter than your goal and says so.
  • Fast runs at 100% power and Gentle at 50%. Both deliver the same dose, so pick whichever fits your time.
  • Rounding: every session time is rounded up to the next half minute, so 2:47 becomes 3 min. The Session Timer, the Control Panel and the Protocol Card use exactly the same time as the cards.
  • Likely under each time is the range it could fall in. The range widens when the panel numbers are a manufacturer claim, when a solar meter correction is applied, and when the distance is outside your readings.
  • Warnings appear under the options. Read them, especially cautions for brain and core treatments.
  • Protocol Card makes a shareable image of the protocol.
Simple View and Lab View
  • Simple View shows the panel, treatment, distance, time, the recommendation and the timer.
  • Lab View (Advanced) adds the dose slider, a depth override, the share of light reaching the target, the irradiance at the surface, a chart and table of power against time, and your panel's evenness map.
  • Both views use the same settings, so changes carry over.
Routines (playlists of protocols)
  • Open them from the Calculator tab, then the Routines sub tab. A routine is a playlist of up to 10 protocols that run back to back, for example Face, then Neck, then Left Knee, then Right Knee.
  • Preset routines: the picker also lists ready-made routines: Leg Recovery, Back and Shoulder Recovery, Knee Care, Shoulder and Elbow Care, Face, Neck and Chest Skin Care, Scalp Hair Regrowth, and Acne: Face, Chest and Upper Back. Each is assembled from the app's own protocols, with Left and Right steps where an area comes in pairs. Open About This Routine to see each treatment's dose window, usual frequency and evidence rating from the Research tab. Distances and times are worked out from your active panel. Presets cannot be changed, so tap Copy to My Routines to make your own version.
  • Build one: tap New, name it, add optional notes and pick the pause between steps. Then set up a protocol on the Calculator and tap Add to Routine (or Add Current Protocol on the Routines page). Each step keeps its treatment, area, distance, power option and side, and the times are worked out from your panel's readings.
  • Customize: every step can be switched between Fast and Gentle and, for areas that come in pairs, between Left, Right and Both. Use Up and Down to reorder, Copy to repeat a step, Replace to swap it for what is set up now, Lock Distance to fix a step at the distance recommended today instead of letting it follow your panel, and Remove to drop it. Edit renames the routine or changes the pause.
  • Run it: tap Start Routine. Each step shows where to place the panel and counts down, with a beep when it ends. Between steps there is a pause so you can move, or you can choose to tap Next yourself. Pause, Skip Step and Stop Routine are always available.
  • Panel control: with a panel connected on the Control Panel tab, tick Run the Panel for Each Step. The app sends one step at a time, so the panel is off during pauses and stops if the app stops checking in. Pause turns the panel off and Resume continues with the time that is left.
  • Every finished step is logged with its side and a note naming the routine and step. Pick the log with Log to before you start.
  • A routine and the single Session Timer cannot run at the same time. While a routine runs, a bar at the bottom lets you jump back to it from other tabs.
Run a session
  • The Session Timer sits under the recommendation. Choose Fast or Gentle, then tap Start.
  • It beeps and vibrates when a part ends or the session finishes. Turn the volume up and the silent switch off. Keep the screen on, because phones can pause timers when the screen locks.
  • Two-wavelength sessions run each wavelength in turn.
  • Stop and log saves the time you actually ran. Finishing the timer saves the whole session automatically.
  • Use Log to to choose which log the session goes in. For areas that come in pairs, such as the knee or shoulder, a Side choice (Left, Right or Both) appears next to it and is saved with the session.

Session Logs
  • Open them from the gear, then Session Logs. Every finished session is saved with treatment, area, side, panel, light, distance, power, minutes and dose. Timer sessions, Control Panel sessions and each step of a routine all log automatically. Routine steps carry a note with the routine name and step number.
  • Log profiles keep separate logs, for example Panel and Mask. Use the picker with New and Edit to add, rename or delete one (deleting also deletes its sessions). Choose where a session goes with Log to on the timer, the Control Panel and the routine runner.

Back up, share and import
  • Share: panel profiles, routines and session logs can go straight to a text message, Messenger or any other app. Tap Share This Routine on the Routines page, Share This Profile in a saved profile's Edit window, or Share on the Session Logs page (it shares what you are viewing).   • The link carries the data inside it, after the # sign, so it is never sent to a server. A very long item, such as a large log, is too big for a message link, so the share sheet sends it as a file instead, or copies the text.
  • If you installed the app on your iPhone, links open in the browser, which keeps its own saved data. Copy the link from the message, open the installed app, tap Import and paste the link into the box. It reads the link for you.

A CSV from before log profiles existed goes into the log you are viewing.
